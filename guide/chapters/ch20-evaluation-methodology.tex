\chapter{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{ch:evaluation-methodology}

\textit{This chapter describes how \sysname{}'s entity resolution pipeline
is evaluated: the synthetic dataset, the train/test split, the metrics,
the ablation study, and reproducibility analysis.  All numbers reported
here are verified against the data files in
\codefile{apps/api/evaluation/data/v5/}.}


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Synthetic Dataset}
\label{sec:eval-synthetic-dataset}

The evaluation uses 200 synthetic developer--AI conversations generated by
\codefile{evaluation/generate\_conversations.py}.  Each conversation
simulates a developer discussing a technology decision with a coding
assistant, and is designed to contain diverse entity surface forms:
abbreviations, aliases, version-qualified names, colloquial references,
and occasional misspellings.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Dataset characteristics:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{200 conversations} across 9 domains: web development,
          backend, DevOps, ML, mobile, data engineering, systems,
          security, and cloud architecture.
    \item \textbf{3,070 entity mentions} extracted and annotated for
          ground-truth canonical names.
    \item Surface-form variants generated by
          \codefile{synthetic\_benchmark.py} include: lowercase, uppercase,
          known abbreviations, version-qualified forms (e.g.\
          ``PostgreSQL~16''), misspellings (character swaps, drops,
          doubles), and suffix variations.
\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Why synthetic?}  Three reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility.}  Anyone can regenerate the exact same
          dataset from the source code, eliminating the need to share
          proprietary conversation logs.
    \item \textbf{No IRB required.}  Synthetic conversations contain no
          real user data, avoiding human subjects research ethics review.
    \item \textbf{Domain control.}  Conversations can be designed to
          cover specific entity types, relationship patterns, and edge
          cases that might be rare in organic data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Train/Test Split}
\label{sec:eval-split}

The 200 conversations are split into two non-overlapping sets:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training set (140 conversations).}  Used to build the
          canonical dictionary and tune resolution thresholds.  The 534
          canonical mappings in \codefile{ontology.py} were developed
          against this set.
    \item \textbf{Held-out test set (60 conversations).}  Used to compute
          all reported metrics.  No information from the test set was used
          to build or tune the resolution pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Non-circularity.}  The split is designed to
prevent data leaks.  The canonical dictionary was frozen before any test
set evaluation.  This means test-set surface forms that happen to be
missing from the dictionary must be resolved by the fuzzy, embedding, or
create-new stages---not by dictionary lookup.  The benchmark CSV produced
by \codefile{synthetic\_benchmark.py} includes a \ic{split} column
(\ic{train}/\ic{test}) to enforce this boundary programmatically.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:eval-metrics}

\subsection{B-cubed Precision, Recall, and F1}

Entity resolution is evaluated using \textbf{B-cubed} metrics, which
measure clustering quality at the level of individual mentions.  For each
mention $m$:

\begin{align}
    \text{Precision}(m) &= \frac{|\text{predicted cluster}(m) \cap
        \text{true cluster}(m)|}{|\text{predicted cluster}(m)|} \\[4pt]
    \text{Recall}(m) &= \frac{|\text{predicted cluster}(m) \cap
        \text{true cluster}(m)|}{|\text{true cluster}(m)|}
\end{align}

Overall precision and recall are the averages across all mentions, and F1
is their harmonic mean.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Worked example with 5 mentions.}
Suppose we have 5 mentions of two entities:

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{l l l}
\toprule
\textbf{Mention} & \textbf{True cluster} & \textbf{Predicted cluster} \\
\midrule
``postgres''     & PostgreSQL & PostgreSQL \\
``pg''           & PostgreSQL & PostgreSQL \\
``PostgreSQL''   & PostgreSQL & PostgreSQL \\
``mongo''        & MongoDB    & MongoDB \\
``Mongo DB''     & MongoDB    & PostgreSQL \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The last mention is a resolution error: ``Mongo DB'' was incorrectly
assigned to the PostgreSQL cluster.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Predicted PostgreSQL cluster:} \{postgres, pg,
          PostgreSQL, Mongo DB\} --- 4 members.
    \item \textbf{True PostgreSQL cluster:} \{postgres, pg, PostgreSQL\}
          --- 3 members.
    \item For mention ``postgres'': Precision = $3/4 = 0.75$, Recall =
          $3/3 = 1.0$.
    \item \textbf{Predicted MongoDB cluster:} \{mongo\} --- 1 member.
    \item \textbf{True MongoDB cluster:} \{mongo, Mongo DB\} --- 2
          members.
    \item For mention ``mongo'': Precision = $1/1 = 1.0$, Recall =
          $1/2 = 0.5$.
\end{itemize}

Averaging across all 5 mentions gives the final B-cubed scores.

\subsection{McNemar's Test}

To determine whether one pipeline configuration is statistically
significantly better than another, we use \textbf{McNemar's test}.  This
compares paired binary outcomes (correct/incorrect) between two systems
on the same test set and produces a p-value indicating whether the
difference is likely due to chance.

\subsection{Expected Calibration Error (ECE)}

The pipeline assigns a confidence score to each entity resolution.
\textbf{ECE} measures whether these confidence scores are well-calibrated:
does a prediction with 90\% confidence actually resolve correctly 90\% of
the time?

ECE is computed by binning predictions by confidence, then measuring the
gap between average confidence and average accuracy within each bin:

\[
    \text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{|B_b|}{N}
    \left| \text{acc}(B_b) - \text{conf}(B_b) \right|
\]

Lower ECE indicates better calibration.  A perfectly calibrated system
has ECE~$= 0$.

\section{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

The ablation study evaluates 7 pipeline configurations, each disabling
one stage of the entity resolution cascade to measure its contribution:

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Ablation configurations.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l X}
\toprule
\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
Full pipeline         & All 7 stages enabled (baseline). \\
No cache              & Disable Redis cache lookup (stage~1). \\
No exact match        & Disable case-insensitive exact match (stage~2). \\
No canonical          & Disable canonical dictionary lookup (stage~3). \\
No alias              & Disable alias search (stage~4). \\
No fuzzy              & Disable fuzzy matching with rapidfuzz (stage~5). \\
No embedding          & Disable embedding similarity (stage~6). \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The ablation script (\codefile{evaluation/ablation.py}) uses
monkeypatching to disable individual stages.  When disabling a stage, the
pipeline falls through to later stages, measuring how much accuracy each
stage contributes.

\begin{warning}
When monkeypatching for ablation, the cache mock needs all three methods
(\ic{get\_by\_exact\_name}, \ic{set\_by\_exact\_name},
\ic{set\_by\_alias}).  The fuzzy stage mock must return \ic{None} (not a
tuple).  The embedding stage is disabled by replacing the embedding service
entirely.
\end{warning}

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:eval-reproducibility}

Because the pipeline uses an LLM (NVIDIA Nemotron) for decision
extraction, results are inherently non-deterministic even with a fixed
temperature setting.  We ran the full extraction pipeline twice on the
same 200 conversations to measure variance.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Run 1 vs.\ Run 2 comparison.}
\label{tab:reproducibility}
\begin{tabular}{l r r r}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Run 1} & \textbf{Run 2} & \textbf{Delta} \\
\midrule
Total decisions              & 364   & 383   & +19 \\
Extraction rate              & 82.5\%& 85.5\%& +3.0\% \\
Conversations with decisions & 165   & 171   & +6 \\
Avg decisions per conv       & 1.82  & 1.92  & +0.10 \\
Total entity links           & 1,292 & 1,282 & $-10$ \\
Avg extraction time (s)      & 28.51 & 26.8  & $-1.71$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Per-conversation agreement.}  Of 200
conversations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{111 (55.5\%)} produced exactly the same number of
          decisions in both runs.
    \item \textbf{155 (77.5\%)} were within $\pm 1$ decision of each
          other.
\end{itemize}

The 77.5\% within-$\pm 1$ rate suggests that while the LLM introduces
meaningful variance at the individual conversation level, aggregate
metrics are reasonably stable across runs.  This is a known property of
LLM-based extraction: the system is stochastic, not deterministic.

\section{Limitations of the Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval-limitations}

Two significant limitations affect the evaluation methodology:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic-only data.}  All 200 conversations are
          synthetically generated, not captured from real developer
          interactions.  Synthetic conversations may not exhibit the full
          range of ambiguity, typos, context-switching, and implicit
          references found in organic conversations.  Metrics computed on
          synthetic data are likely \emph{optimistic} compared to what
          would be observed on real data.

    \item \textbf{Single primary annotator.}  Ground-truth labels for
          entity mentions were produced by one annotator (the system
          developer).  This introduces potential bias, particularly for
          ambiguous cases where the annotator's domain knowledge may
          influence labeling decisions.  Inter-annotator agreement was not
          measured.  Ideally, at least two independent annotators would
          label the data, with disagreements resolved by a third.
\end{enumerate}

These limitations are discussed further in
Chapter~\ref{ch:known-limitations}, which covers the broader set of
constraints affecting the system as a whole.
